How much do HVAC techs charge per hour?

Independent HVAC technicians commonly charge somewhere around $75 to $150 an hour, plus a diagnostic fee - and more in peak season. Here is what drives the number, and how to set a rate that holds up when the calls pile in.

By the TradeReady team Published Aug 19, 2026 Last reviewed Aug 19, 2026 8 min read

HVAC pricing confuses customers more than most trades, because the hourly rate is only part of the bill - there's usually a diagnostic fee, sometimes peak-season pricing, and a parts markup on top. None of it is a trick. It's what happens when a certified technician's real costs meet a business that's slammed in July and quiet in October.

This guide gives you a realistic range to check against, then walks the method for building your own rate. It's the same approach as our general guide to setting an hourly rate and the whole-job version in how to price a job, pointed at HVAC.

The typical range for an independent HVAC tech

As a market check, solo and small-shop HVAC technicians in the US tend to sit here:

Hourly labor rate$75–$150 / hr
Diagnostic / service-call fee$75–$200
After-hours or emergency1.5×–2× rate
Peak-season demandcan lift rates further

A technician doing routine maintenance and filter changes sits toward the bottom. One handling compressor swaps, refrigerant work, and full system installs sits toward the top. Where you land depends on your certification, your costs, and how hard the season is running - which the next section builds.

Why HVAC rates carry so much

Before any of the hourly rate is wage, it has to cover:

  • EPA 608 certification - required to handle refrigerant legally, plus any state or local licensing.
  • Specialized equipment - gauges, recovery machines, vacuum pumps, combustion analyzers, and leak detectors that aren't cheap to buy or replace.
  • Insurance and a stocked van - capacitors, contactors, motors, and common parts so you fix it on the first trip.
  • Seasonal swing - a business that's overwhelmed in summer and slow in the shoulder months has to earn a full year's income across an uneven calendar.
  • Unbillable hours - driving, quoting, parts runs, and callbacks.

A solo tech might work 45 hours in a peak week and bill 25 to 30. All of the above has to be recovered across the billable hours, and in the slow months there are fewer of them - which is why the charge-out rate runs well above the take-home wage.

Build your rate from your own costs

The short version (the full billable-hours math is in how much to charge per hour):

  1. Set your take-home target - the annual wage the business owes you.
  2. Count your real billable hours - the hours you invoice across the whole year, peak and slow. Often 1,000 to 1,300 for a solo operator.
  3. Total your yearly overhead - certification, insurance, van, fuel, instruments, software, phone - and spread it across those billable hours.
  4. Wage per hour plus overhead per hour = your cost rate - the floor.
  5. Add profit on top. The margin above cost is the part that gets you ahead.
Why certified trades sit higher

Our general example lands a solo operator at a $60/hr cost rate. A certified HVAC tech usually lands higher - the instruments are expensive, the certification and insurance cost real money, and the seasonal swing means fewer billable hours to spread it all across. That's a higher genuine cost of doing the work, and your rate has to carry it.

The diagnostic fee is not optional

HVAC is the trade where a service or diagnostic fee matters most. A technician can spend an hour with gauges and a meter tracking down why a system won't cool - real, skilled work - and if there's no diagnostic fee and the customer declines the repair, that hour pays nothing. A fixed diagnostic fee covers the trip, the instruments, and the time to find the fault. Crediting it toward the repair when the customer proceeds keeps it fair and still protects you when they don't.

Flat-rate and maintenance plans

Many HVAC shops quote common jobs - a capacitor replacement, a tune-up, a system install - at a flat price, and sell maintenance plans that smooth the seasonal swing into steady income. Both are still built on your hourly cost rate: estimate the labor honestly, multiply by the rate you built above, add parts with a sensible markup, and you have a number that pays whether it's August or February. Set the parts side with markup vs. margin first.

The bottom line

"How much do HVAC techs charge per hour?" has no single answer, because the number carries certification, expensive instruments, and a seasonal calendar on top of a wage. Build yours from real cost plus real overhead divided by the hours you can bill across the whole year, add profit, and always charge a diagnostic fee so skilled troubleshooting isn't free. Use the market range only to confirm you're in the right ballpark - never to set your price.

Common questions

How much do HVAC techs charge per hour?

Independent HVAC technicians in the US commonly charge somewhere around $75 to $150 an hour, plus a diagnostic or service-call fee. Peak-season and after-hours work often runs higher. The right rate for you is the one built from your own costs, not the market average.

Why do HVAC companies charge a diagnostic fee?

The diagnostic fee covers the trip, the gauges and instruments, and the time to find the actual fault before any repair is quoted. Without it, a technician can spend an hour diagnosing a system and, if the customer declines the repair, get paid nothing for real work. Many shops credit the fee toward the repair if the customer proceeds.

Why is HVAC labor more expensive in summer?

Demand spikes when it is hot, and after-hours and weekend calls carry premium rates because the technician is giving up personal time and responding fast. Peak-season and emergency pricing also help ration limited hours toward the most urgent work.
