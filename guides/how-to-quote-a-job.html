How to quote a job: quote vs. estimate, with examples

A quote is a promise of a price. Before you make one, you need to know what you're promising to do, what it costs you, and how you'll handle it if the job changes.

By the TradeReady team Published Sep 29, 2026 Last reviewed Sep 29, 2026 9 min read

A customer describes the job over the phone and asks, "What would you charge?" You have about three ways to answer. You can guess a number and hope, you can say "I'll have to come look," or you can quote it properly. The first one is how tradespeople end up working for free. The second is fine, but only if what comes next is a real price. This guide is about that real price: how to work one out, how to word it, and whether to call it a quote or an estimate.

Quote vs. estimate: what's the difference?

The two words are used loosely, and in some places they're treated as interchangeable. In everyday trade talk, though, there's a useful distinction:

Quote vs. estimate in everyday use
  Quote Estimate
What it isA fixed price for a defined jobYour best judgment of the likely price, often a range
Is the number firm?Yes, unless the scope changesNo, it can move as the work unfolds
Who carries the risk of a surprise?YouShared, or the customer
Best whenThe scope is clear and you've done this job beforeThe scope is uncertain or open-ended
Watch the wording

How a quote or an estimate is treated, including whether you can charge more than you said, depends on your contract and your local rules, and the terms aren't used the same way everywhere. Whatever you call it, write down what it covers and how you'll handle changes, and check what applies where you work.

When to give a quote, and when to give an estimate

Match the document to how much you actually know:

  • Give a quote when the scope is clear and the work is familiar. You can see it, you've done it before, and you know the time and the materials. A fixed price is easier for the customer and rewards you for doing it efficiently. This is the same idea as flat-rate pricing.
  • Give an estimate when something is genuinely unknown: what's behind the wall, how bad the rot is, what the customer will decide about finishes. A range is honest. A single number would just be a guess wearing a suit.
  • Diagnose first when you can't say either. Charge for a look, then quote the fix. See minimum and call-out fees.

How to quote a price for a job, step by step

  1. Understand the job. Ask questions, get photos, or visit. What's being done, where, what condition is it in, and what does the customer expect the end result to look like? Quoting from a doorway description is where most bad quotes begin.
  2. Write the scope, and what's not included. Say what you'll do and what you won't. An "exclusions" line stops "I assumed that was included" before it starts.
  3. Cost it properly. Labor hours at your rate, materials with your markup, travel, overhead, and your target margin. The full method is in how to price a job. A quote is just that number, written down and committed to.
  4. Add a cushion where there's real uncertainty. An old house or awkward access deserves extra time in the hours, not a hopeful shrug.
  5. Choose how to present it. One firm price, a range, or a few options at different levels. See good-better-best pricing for the last one.
  6. Add the terms. How long the price is good for, the deposit and payment schedule (see deposits and payment terms), and how changes are handled (see change orders).
  7. Send it quickly and follow up. A clear written quote that arrives the same day looks professional, and a short check-in a few days later often gets a reply that silence never would.

A worked example: replacing a bathroom vanity

Say you're replacing a vanity, including a new faucet and supply lines. You expect it to take about five hours. The parts cost you $220 for the vanity, $85 for the faucet, and $45 for supply lines and shutoff valves. You use a $70 labor rate, a 20% materials markup, 15% overhead, and a 20% target margin.

Pricing the vanity replacement
Labor (5 hours at $70)$350.00
Materials ($350 at cost, 20% markup)$420.00
Subtotal$770.00
Overhead (15% of subtotal)$115.50
Cost basis$885.50
Price (cost basis ÷ 0.80)$1,106.88

That's the number. Now you decide how to present it. If the job is clear and familiar, you quote it. If the customer might change their mind, or the plumbing behind the old vanity is unknown, you give an estimate range built from a shorter and a longer time on the job, with the parts price held firm:

Quote5$1,106.88, fixed, for the scope listed

Notice that the range comes from the same math, run twice with different hours. You aren't inventing two numbers. The estimate is honest about the hours you can't yet see, and the quote commits to the hours you expect.

What a good written quote includes

  • The customer's name, the address, and the date.
  • A plain description of the work, and what's excluded.
  • The price, and whether it includes materials, permits, and cleanup.
  • How long the price is valid, so a slow reply doesn't hold you to old material costs.
  • The deposit and payment terms.
  • How you'll handle changes to the scope.

Common quoting mistakes

  • Quoting sight unseen. A number from a description can't include what you haven't learned yet.
  • Vague scope. "Redo the bathroom" can mean five different jobs.
  • Forgetting the small costs. Disposal, permits, delivery, and the second trip for a forgotten part.
  • No expiry date. Materials move. An open-ended quote becomes a free option for the customer.
  • Quoting and disappearing. The job often goes to whoever follows up.

The bottom line

Quote when you can see the whole job and estimate when you can't. Either way, do the real costing first, write down what's included and excluded, put an expiry date and the terms on it, and follow up. A quote isn't a guess you're hoping to win. It's a price you've worked out and are happy to do the job for.

Common questions

What is the difference between a quote and an estimate?

In everyday trade use, a quote is a fixed price for a defined job, while an estimate is your best judgment of the likely price, often given as a range, that can move as the work unfolds. The words are used loosely and are treated differently in different places, so write down what your document covers and check what applies where you work.

How do I quote a price for a job?

Understand the job first, then write the scope and what's excluded, and cost it with labor hours at your rate, materials with markup, travel, overhead, and your target margin. Add a cushion where there's real uncertainty, decide whether to present one price, a range, or options, and include an expiry date and payment terms. Send it promptly and follow up.

Can I charge more than my estimate?

That depends on what you agreed and on local rules, so check what applies to you. As a habit, say in writing how changes will be handled, and use a range or a diagnostic visit when the scope is uncertain. If the scope changes after you've agreed a price, document it as a change order rather than absorbing it or adding a surprise at the end.

How long should a quote be valid?

There's no standard period. Choose a window that reflects how quickly your material prices and your availability change, and state it on the quote. A stated expiry date protects you from being held to old prices if the customer takes a long time to decide.

Should I charge for giving a quote?

That's your call. Many tradespeople quote simple jobs for free, and some charge for a site visit on larger or more complex work, crediting the fee if the customer goes ahead. Decide on a policy, tell customers before the visit, and apply it consistently.

What should a job quote include?

The customer's details and the date, a plain description of the work and what's excluded, the price and what it covers, how long it's valid, the deposit and payment terms, and how changes to the scope will be handled. A copy-ready template is in the job estimate guide.
