How to price a drywall job

Drywall looks like it should be simple to price - so many sheets, so much wall. But it is really three jobs stacked in one quote, spread across days of drying, and the money leaks in the stages and the return trips people forget to charge for. Here is how to price it properly, with a room costed end to end.

By the TradeReady team Published Aug 22, 2026 Last reviewed Aug 22, 2026 10 min read

Drywall is one of those trades that looks priceable at a glance - count the sheets, add some mud, name a number. That glance is exactly how a finisher ends up on the third coat of compound three days later, sanding dust out of their hair, working for less than the job cost. Hanging board is fast and visible. The money is decided in the finishing, the texture, and the days between coats that nobody sees.

Drywall isn't priced by different math from any other trade - it is the same four parts (labor, materials, overhead, and profit) behind every price. What makes drywall its own problem is the shape of the work: it is really three jobs in one, it is measured per sheet or per square foot, and it is stretched across drying time that adds days and trips without adding billable hours. Get those three things straight and the price behaves. This guide walks through each, then prices a room from bare studs to a finished wall.

Why drywall pricing is its own problem

The first trap is that drywall is three jobs wearing one name: hanging the board, taping and finishing the joints, and applying texture. Hanging is fast. Finishing is slow, skilled, and where the quality lives. Texture is its own step again. A customer hears "drywall" and pictures a smooth, paint-ready wall; a careless quote covers only the hanging. That gap is where most drywall pricing arguments are born.

The second trap is drying time. Joint compound has to dry between coats, and a proper finish is several coats. That means the job spans days you are not working and needs multiple trips back to the site. You cannot bill the drying, but each return trip costs you a drive, a setup, and a cleanup. Price the hours you work and the trips you make - not the calendar days, and never zero for the trips.

The four parts, drywall-style

Every honest price is built from the same four blocks. If you have read how to price a job, this is the same skeleton - here is what each part looks like for drywall:

  1. Labor - your time across all the stages you are doing: hanging, taping, coating, sanding, texturing, and the setup and cleanup on every return trip. Finishing is the big, skilled chunk; count it in full.
  2. Materials - sheets, joint compound, tape, screws, corner bead, and the sanding gear - at cost, plus a markup for sourcing and handling. Add a waste allowance for the cuts.
  3. Overhead - the share of your monthly running costs the job should carry: insurance, vehicle, phone, tools, and dust control.
  4. Profit - what the business earns after all three above are paid, including paying you. Your wage is a cost; profit sits on top.

Add the first three to get your cost. Add profit to get your price. The rest of this guide is about counting the labor honestly, because on drywall that is where the number is won or lost.

Hang, finish, texture: say what your price covers

Before you quote, decide and state which of the three stages the price includes. The three are genuinely different amounts of work:

  • Hanging - lifting, cutting, and screwing the board to the framing. Fast, physical, and the cheapest stage per square foot.
  • Taping and finishing - taping the joints and applying, drying, and sanding multiple coats of compound until the wall is smooth. Slow, skilled, spread over days, and usually the largest slice of the labor.
  • Texture - spraying or troweling a finish over the wall, then any needed touch-up. A separate step with its own materials and its own cleanup.

Quote the stages you are actually doing, name them in the estimate, and treat anything you are not doing as a hand-off, not an assumption. "Hang only" and "hang and finish to a smooth wall" are different prices for a reason.

Levels of finish: what the customer is really buying

How smooth the finished wall needs to be is a real variable, not a matter of pride. The trades describe it as levels of finish - a scale from a basic taped joint up to a fully skim-coated surface ready for critical, raking light. Each step up the scale is more coats of compound, more sanding, and more time, so it costs more.

The point for pricing is simple: agree the level before you quote. A garage, a ceiling in a utility room, or a wall that will sit behind cabinets does not need the same finish as a feature wall under a big window - and pricing every wall to the highest standard, or quoting a high wall to a low-level price, both cost you. Match the finish to the job, and let the level drive the finishing hours.

Match the finish to the light and the coating

A wall getting a heavy texture hides more than a wall getting a flat, glossy paint under raking light. The smoother the final coating and the harsher the light, the higher the finish level - and the more finishing hours - the job needs. Ask what is going on the wall and where the light comes from before you price the finish, not after.

Measuring: sheets, square footage, and waste

Start from the area. Work out the square footage of wall and ceiling to be boarded, the same way you would for paint: wall lengths times height, plus length times width for ceilings. From there you can price per square foot, or divide by the area of a standard sheet to price per sheet - they are the same number expressed two ways.

Then add waste. Cuts, offcuts, and the odd damaged board mean you buy more material than the bare area suggests; a waste allowance of roughly 10–15% is a common, illustrative planning figure, higher on a chopped-up room with lots of angles and openings. Per-sheet pricing is quick and clean on a new-build room full of full boards; per square foot travels better when the job is all cuts and partial walls. Whichever you show the customer, the hours behind it are what actually set the price.

Worked example: hang and finish a room

Let's price hanging and finishing a small room to a standard smooth finish - board it out, tape and coat the joints over a few days, sand, and leave it ready for the painter. We'll use a $40/hr take-home wage, $20/hr overhead, a 25% materials markup, and a 25% profit margin. (Setting those numbers for yourself is its own topic - how much to charge per hour walks through it.)

Say the room takes about sixteen billable hours all in - hanging the board, three coats of compound with sanding, and the setup and cleanup on the return trips the drying forces. Materials - sheets, compound, tape, screws, and bead, with a waste allowance - come to $300 at cost.

Hang & finish a small room to a smooth finish · ~16 hours across return trips
Labor - 16 hrs × $40 wage$640.00
Overhead - 16 hrs × $20$320.00
Materials - $300 cost + 25% markup$375.00
Cost of the job$1,335.00
Your profit on the job$445.00

Illustrative example — figures chosen to show the method, not a quote. How we source figures.

So you quote $1,780. Your $640 wage is paid, your $320 of overhead is covered, your materials come back with a fair markup, and the business clears $445 in profit - a true 25% margin on the price. To get there you divide, not add: cost ÷ (1 − margin), so $1,335 ÷ 0.75 = $1,780. Multiplying $1,335 by 1.25 would leave you a hair short of a real margin - the difference that markup vs. margin is all about.

Now find the leak. Price this as "hanging" and quietly assume the finishing will "only take a couple of hours," and you might book six or eight hours instead of sixteen. That is $400 to $600 of labor and overhead gone - the exact hours the customer is paying for when they picture a smooth wall. The finishing didn't get faster because you hoped it would.

The dead-time trap

Drywall's most expensive mistake is confusing elapsed time with billable time, and it cuts both ways. Don't bill the customer for the days the compound sits drying while you are on another job - you are not working those hours. But don't give away the consequences of that drying either: the job needs several visits, and each visit is a drive, an unload, a setup, and a cleanup before and after the actual coating.

Count the trips into your labor. A three-coat finish can mean three or more trips to the same room, and if you only priced the hours "on the wall" you have donated all the driving and setup around them. Price the work you do and the visits it takes; ignore the calendar days in between.

Repairs and patches: price with a minimum

A small patch is the drywall version of the small-job trap, and it is worse than it looks. A single hole still needs a trip, a piece of board cut and fitted, tape, several coats of compound with drying time between them, sanding, and then matching the existing texture - often the fiddliest part of the whole thing - usually across two or three visits. The square footage is nothing; the effort is real.

So never price a patch off its size. Set a minimum job price that covers the trips, the setup, and the texture matching, and hold it. If a customer has several small patches, do them in one round of visits so the trips are shared - that is the honest way to make small drywall work pay.

How drywallers underprice (and how to stop)

  • Pricing the hang, forgetting the finish. Taping and coating is usually the biggest slice of labor. Price every stage you are doing, and say which stages the quote covers.
  • Not counting the return trips. Drying forces multiple visits, each with its own drive, setup, and cleanup. Put those hours in - don't donate them to the drying.
  • Quoting one level of finish, delivering a higher one. A smooth wall under raking light is far more sanding than a taped garage. Agree the level before you quote.
  • Skipping the waste allowance. Cuts and offcuts mean you buy more board than the bare area. Build in a waste percentage or you buy the difference out of profit.
  • Pricing patches by their size. A tiny hole is nearly all setup, trips, and texture matching. Use a minimum job price and bundle patches into shared visits.

The bottom line

Drywall pricing comes down to counting the labor honestly across a job that hides most of it. Name the stages your price covers, agree the level of finish before you quote, and count the finishing hours and the return trips the drying forces - not the calendar days it takes. Do that, add a real waste allowance and a minimum for patches, and the trade that looks like counting sheets turns into a business that actually pays for the days you spend on it.

Common questions

Is drywall priced per sheet or per square foot?

Both are common, and they are two ways of saying the same thing - a standard sheet is a known area, so a per-sheet price is just a per-square-foot price rolled up. Per sheet is quick for a straightforward new-build room with lots of full boards. Per square foot travels better across jobs with lots of cuts, angles, or partial walls. Whichever you quote the customer, build the number from your own hours and materials underneath, and be clear about whether the price covers hang only, or hang and finish, or finish and texture too.

Does a drywall price include hanging, finishing, and texture?

Only if you say so. Drywall is really three jobs - hanging the board, taping and finishing the joints, and texturing - and each can be priced on its own. A lot of pricing disputes come from a customer hearing one price and picturing a finished, painted-ready wall while the quote only covered hanging. State exactly which stages your price includes, and price any stage you are not doing as a separate line or hand-off.

What is a level of finish in drywall?

Levels of finish are an industry way of describing how smooth and refined the finished surface is, from a basic taped joint up to a fully skim-coated wall ready for critical lighting. A higher level means more coats of compound, more sanding, and more time, so it costs more. The level a job needs depends on what goes on the wall and how the light hits it - a garage or a wall behind cabinets does not need the same finish as a feature wall under raking light. Agree the level before you quote, because it changes the labor.

Should I charge for drying time between coats?

No - you cannot bill hours you are not working, and joint compound needs time to dry between coats whether you are there or not. What you must price is the effect of that drying: the job spans several days and needs multiple return trips, and each trip carries its own drive, setup, and cleanup. Price the hours you actually work plus those return visits, not the calendar days the job takes.

How should I price a small drywall repair or patch?

Price it with a minimum, because a small patch is almost all setup. A single hole still needs a trip, materials, cutting and fitting a patch, several coats of compound with drying time between them, sanding, and matching the existing texture - often across two or three visits. The square footage is tiny but the effort is not. Set a minimum job price that covers turning up and the return trips, and never quote a patch off its size alone.
