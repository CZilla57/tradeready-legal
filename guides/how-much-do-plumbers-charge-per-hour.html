How much do plumbers charge per hour?

Independent plumbers commonly charge somewhere around $75 to $150 an hour - but the average tells you almost nothing about what you should charge. Here is what sits behind the number, and how to set a rate that actually pays.

By the TradeReady team Published Aug 18, 2026 Last reviewed Aug 19, 2026 8 min read

If you search "how much do plumbers charge per hour," you'll find figures anywhere from $50 to $200. Both can be right, in different vans, in different towns. The spread is so wide because the hourly rate isn't a price tag - it's the output of one plumber's costs divided by the hours they can actually bill.

This guide gives you a realistic range to sanity-check against, then shows how to build your own number from the ground up. The method is the same one in our general guide to setting an hourly rate and the whole-job walkthrough in how to price a job - this page just points it at plumbing.

The typical range for an independent plumber

As a rough market check, solo and small-shop plumbers in the US tend to land in this territory:

Typical independent plumber pricing (US)
Hourly labor rate$75–$150 / hr
Service-call / minimum fee$50–$150
Emergency or after-hours1.5×–2× rate
Apprentice / helper rateoften billed lower

Treat those as the shape of the market, not your price. A master plumber with a stocked van in a high-cost city sits at the top; someone doing straightforward repairs in a rural area sits lower. Where you land depends on your costs and your local demand, which is exactly what the next section builds.

Why plumber rates look high (they mostly aren't)

Customers see "$120 an hour" and picture the plumber pocketing $120. They don't see what the number has to cover before any of it is wage:

  • Licensing and continuing education - the ticket that lets you legally do the work, kept current.
  • Liability insurance and bond - non-negotiable, and priced for water damage risk.
  • A stocked van - fittings, valves, torch, camera, augers, press tools. The rolling inventory that means you fix it on the first visit.
  • Unbillable hours - driving, quoting, parts runs, permits, and chasing payment. None of it bills, all of it is time.

That last one is the big one. A plumber might work a 45-hour week but bill only 25 to 30 hours of it. Every dollar of wage, insurance, and van stock has to be recovered across those billable hours - so the rate per billable hour has to run well above the wage you take home.

Build your rate from your own costs

Here's the short version of the method (the long version, with the billable-hours math, is in how much to charge per hour):

  1. Set your take-home target - the wage this business owes you for a year.
  2. Count your real billable hours - not the hours you work, the hours you invoice. For a solo plumber that's often 1,000 to 1,300 a year.
  3. Add up your yearly overhead - insurance, van, fuel, tools, licensing, phone, software - and spread it across those same billable hours.
  4. Wage per hour plus overhead per hour = your cost rate - the floor. Charge exactly this and you break even.
  5. Add profit on top. The gap above your cost rate is the only part that actually gets you ahead.
Why licensed trades sit higher

Our general example lands a solo operator at a $60/hr cost rate. A licensed plumber usually lands higher - the insurance is dearer, the van carries more, and the tools cost more to buy and maintain. That's not markup; it's a higher genuine cost of being in business, and your rate has to carry it.

Charge a service call, not just an hourly rate

A pure hourly rate punishes you on small jobs. If a customer 30 minutes away has a leak that takes 20 minutes to fix, one "hour" of billing doesn't come close to covering the drive, the diagnosis, and the van. That's why most plumbers add a service-call or minimum fee: a fixed amount that covers showing up and the first block of time, with the hourly rate running after that. It's not gouging - it's making sure a genuine callout doesn't lose money.

Flat-rate vs. hourly for common jobs

Plenty of plumbers quote familiar jobs - a water heater swap, a faucet replacement, a toilet reset - as a flat price rather than by the hour. The customer likes the certainty, and once you've done a job twenty times you know how long it takes. Flat-rate pricing is still built from your hourly cost rate underneath: estimate the hours honestly, multiply by the rate you built above, add materials with a sensible markup, and you have a flat number that still pays. The bottom line

"How much do plumbers charge per hour?" has no single answer, and the average is a trap. The honest number is the one you calculate: a real wage plus real overhead, divided by the hours you can actually bill, with profit on top. Build it from your own costs, add a service-call fee so small jobs still pay, and use the market range only to check you're in the right ballpark - never to set the price for you.

Common questions

How much do plumbers charge per hour?

Independent plumbers in the US commonly charge somewhere around $75 to $150 an hour, with many also adding a service-call or minimum-visit fee. The range is wide because licensing, insurance, van stock, and local demand vary a lot. The rate that matters is the one built from your own costs, not the average.

Why do plumbers charge so much per hour?

The hourly figure has to cover far more than the time on the tools. A plumber carries licensing, liability insurance, a stocked van, specialized tools, and hours of unbillable driving, quoting, and parts runs. Spread across the fewer hours actually billed, a rate that looks high is often just break-even plus a modest profit.

Should a plumber charge a call-out fee on top of the hourly rate?

Many do. A service-call or minimum fee covers the drive, the diagnosis, and the guaranteed first block of time so a short visit still pays. It protects you on small jobs where an hourly rate alone would not cover the cost of showing up.
