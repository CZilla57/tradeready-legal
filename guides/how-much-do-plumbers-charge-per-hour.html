How much do plumbers charge per hour?

As a rough US ballpark, independent plumbers often bill somewhere around $75 to $150 an hour - but the hourly rate is only one line on the bill. This guide explains how plumber pricing actually fits together, whether you're comparing quotes or setting your own rate.

By the TradeReady team Published Aug 18, 2026 Last reviewed Aug 22, 2026 10 min read

Search "how much do plumbers charge per hour" and you'll see figures from $50 to $200. Both can be right, in different vans and different towns - because the hourly rate isn't a price tag. It's one input into a bill that usually also carries a service-call fee, a minimum charge, materials, and sometimes a flat-rate price that ignores the clock entirely.

Who this guide is for

This page serves two readers, in clearly labeled sections. If you're a customer comparing plumbing charges, start with what plumbers charge and why the bill works the way it does. If you're an independent plumber setting your rate, skip to building a rate that actually pays. Everyone is welcome in both.

Those are the shape of the market, not your quote. A master plumber with a fully stocked van in a high-cost city sits at the top; a straightforward repair in a rural area sits lower. What lands on your invoice depends on the job and the shop.

The parts of a plumbing bill, and what each one means

Almost every confused-looking plumbing invoice makes sense once you know the pieces. They're separate charges, and not every job has all of them:

  • Labor rate - the per-hour price for time on the tools. It's the number people ask about, but rarely the whole bill.
  • Service-call fee (also call-out or trip fee) - a fixed charge for coming out and diagnosing the problem. It pays for the drive and the plumber's time to figure out what's wrong.
  • Minimum charge - the smallest amount a shop will bill for a visit, so a five-minute fix still covers the cost of getting a licensed plumber to your door. Sometimes the minimum is the service-call fee; sometimes it's a first "hour" that's charged whole even if the work takes less.
  • Flat-rate price - a fixed quote for a known job (a water-heater swap, a toilet reset) regardless of how long it actually takes. You get certainty; the clock stops mattering.
  • Materials - parts, fittings, and fixtures, usually billed with a markup that covers sourcing, stocking, and warranty. (Markup and margin aren't the same thing - see markup vs. margin.)
  • Permits - some work legally requires a pulled permit and an inspection; the permit fee and the time to handle it are passed through to you.
  • Emergency premium - the extra charged for off-hours or urgent response, commonly 1.5× to 2× the standard rate.

Why your bill isn't "hours on site × hourly rate"

A common frustration: the plumber was at your house for 40 minutes, the rate is $120/hr, so why is the bill $210? Because you can almost never rebuild a plumbing invoice by multiplying visible onsite time by the hourly rate. The total also carries things you didn't watch happen:

  • a service-call or minimum charge that's a fixed amount, not a fraction of an hour;
  • flat-rate pricing on jobs where the clock is deliberately ignored;
  • materials and fittings with markup;
  • permit or disposal costs passed through;
  • billed travel or a parts run - time spent for your job that happened off your property.

None of that means you were overcharged. It means the hourly rate was only ever one line of several. If a total surprises you, ask for the invoice broken out by these parts - a reputable plumber will walk you through it.

How a service fee and an hourly rate actually interact

This is where quotes get misread. It's tempting to assume the service-call fee stacks on top of a full hourly rate from the first minute. Often it doesn't - the fee usually includes a first block of time, and the hourly clock starts after that block:

$90 service call (covers first 30 min)  +  $120/hr × 45 min after that  =  $90 + $90  =  $180

Compare that with the wrong assumption - fee plus a full hour on top from minute one - which would read $90 + $120 = $210 for the same visit. The gap is the "first block" the fee already bought. Because shops structure this differently, don't assume fees automatically stack: ask whether the service call covers any labor time, and where the hourly rate picks up.

Licensing, bonding, and insurance (varies by where you live)

Part of what a plumber's rate carries is the cost of being licensed, bonded, and insured - and hiring one who is protects you if something goes wrong. But the specifics vary widely: plumbing licensing and bonding requirements are set by each state, and sometimes by the county or city, not by a single national rule. Some places license plumbers directly; others regulate at the municipal level; the classes of license and what each allows differ too. Before hiring, it's reasonable to ask for a license number and proof of insurance, then check it against your state or local licensing board. What's required, and how to verify it, depends entirely on your jurisdiction - treat any general statement here as a prompt to check locally, not a definitive rule.

For plumbers: building a rate that pays

If you're setting your own rate, the market range above is a sanity-check, not a target. The rate that matters is the one built from your costs. The full method - with the billable-hours math - is in our general guide to setting an hourly rate; this section points it at plumbing rather than repeating it.

Why plumber rates look high (they mostly aren't)

Customers see "$120 an hour" and picture the plumber pocketing $120. They don't see what the number has to cover before any of it is wage:

  • Licensing and continuing education - the ticket that lets you legally do the work, kept current.
  • Liability insurance and bond - non-negotiable, and priced for water-damage risk.
  • A stocked van - fittings, valves, torch, camera, augers, press tools. The rolling inventory that means you fix it on the first visit.
  • Unbillable hours - driving, quoting, parts runs, permits, and chasing payment. None of it bills; all of it is time.

That last one is the big one. A plumber might work a 45-hour week but bill only 25 to 30 hours of it. Every dollar of wage, insurance, and van stock has to be recovered across those billable hours - so the rate per billable hour has to run well above the wage you take home.

Build the rate from your own costs

The short version (the full walkthrough, with the billable-hours math, is in how much to charge per hour): take your yearly take-home target, add your yearly overhead, and divide the total by the hours you can actually bill - not the hours you work. That gives your cost rate, the floor. Charge exactly it and you break even; the profit is whatever you add on top.

Why licensed trades sit higher

Our general example lands a solo operator at a $60/hr cost rate. A licensed plumber usually lands higher - the insurance is dearer, the van carries more, and the tools cost more to buy and maintain. That's not markup; it's a higher genuine cost of being in business, and your rate has to carry it.

Charge a service call, not just an hourly rate

A pure hourly rate punishes you on small jobs. If a customer 30 minutes away has a leak that takes 20 minutes to fix, one "hour" of billing doesn't come close to covering the drive, the diagnosis, and the van. That's why most plumbers add a service-call or minimum fee: a fixed amount that covers showing up and the first block of time, with the hourly rate running after that. Decide clearly whether that fee includes a first block of labor or sits beside it, and put it in the quote - the customer confusion in the section above is exactly what an unclear fee structure causes.

Flat-rate vs. hourly for common jobs

Plenty of plumbers quote familiar jobs - a water-heater swap, a faucet replacement, a toilet reset - as a flat price rather than by the hour. The customer likes the certainty, and once you've done a job twenty times you know how long it takes. Flat-rate pricing is still built from your hourly cost rate underneath: estimate the hours honestly, multiply by the rate you built above, add materials with a sensible markup, and you have a flat number that still pays. The bottom line

"How much do plumbers charge per hour?" has no single answer, and the hourly rate alone never tells you the whole cost. For customers, the bill is the labor rate plus a service call, materials, permits, and sometimes a flat price - which is why you can't rebuild it from onsite minutes. For plumbers, the honest rate is a real wage plus real overhead, divided by the hours you can actually bill, with profit on top - and a clear service-call fee so small jobs still pay. Use the market range only to check you're in the right ballpark, never to set the price for you.

Common questions

How much do plumbers charge per hour?

As a rough US ballpark, independent plumbers often bill somewhere around $75 to $150 an hour, and many add a service-call or minimum fee on top of that. This is TradeReady's own marketplace estimate for 2026, not a formal survey, and the figure a plumber charges a customer already has to cover their insurance, van, licensing, and unbillable time - it is not a take-home wage. Your actual quote depends on the job, your location, and the plumber's costs.

What is a plumber's service-call or call-out fee?

A service-call fee (also called a call-out or trip fee) is a fixed charge for coming to your property and diagnosing the problem, commonly in the $50 to $150 range as a rough US estimate. On many jobs it covers the first block of time as well, so it does not always stack on top of a full hourly rate from the first minute - ask the plumber how their fee and hourly rate interact before work starts.

Why is my plumbing bill more than the hours the plumber was at my house times their hourly rate?

Because the hourly rate is only one line of the bill. A typical invoice can also include a service-call or minimum charge, a flat-rate price for the job rather than clock time, materials and fittings with markup, permit or disposal costs, and billed travel or parts-run time you did not see. Multiplying the minutes on site by the hourly rate almost never reproduces the total, and that is normal rather than a sign of overcharging.

Do plumbers charge more for emergencies or after hours?

Usually, yes. Nights, weekends, holidays, and true emergency call-outs are commonly billed at roughly 1.5 to 2 times the standard rate, a rough estimate that varies by shop. The premium pays for interrupting off-hours and responding fast. If the problem can safely wait until normal hours, asking about standard-rate scheduling can lower the cost.

Do I have to pay if the plumber just diagnoses the problem and I do not go ahead?

Often, yes. A diagnostic or service-call fee typically covers the visit and the plumber's time to find the problem, whether or not you approve the repair. Some shops waive or credit that fee toward the work if you proceed. Ask up front whether the visit has a fee and whether it applies to the repair - policies vary by business and are not set by law.
