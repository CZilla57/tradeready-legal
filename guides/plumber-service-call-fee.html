Plumber service call fee: how to set it and explain it

A service call fee pays for the part of the job that happens before you touch a pipe: the drive, the truck, and the time it takes to work out what's wrong. Here's how to set one that covers it.

By the TradeReady team Published Sep 29, 2026 Last reviewed Sep 29, 2026 8 min read

The call comes in: a dripping faucet, "should be quick." You drive across town, find the cause in ten minutes, fix it in fifteen, and bill for a quarter of an hour. The customer is delighted. You've just spent the better part of an hour and earned about a quarter of one. A service call fee exists to close that gap. It pays for you showing up, ready to work, before any repair begins.

What a service call fee is

A service call fee is a set charge for coming out to a property and working out what's wrong, separate from the repair itself. You'll hear it called a call-out fee, a trip charge, a diagnostic fee, or a minimum charge. The names overlap, but they're not always the same thing:

  • Service call or call-out fee - a fixed charge for the visit: the drive, the truck, and the initial diagnosis.
  • Trip charge - often just the travel part of that, sometimes based on distance.
  • Diagnostic fee - specifically for finding the problem, sometimes on top of the trip.
  • Minimum charge - the least you'll bill for any visit, however quick the fix. See minimum job and call-out fees for how it works across trades.

Whichever names you use, make sure you and the customer mean the same thing by them.

What the fee needs to cover

  • The drive. Your time and fuel there and back, and the wear on the van.
  • The diagnosis. Finding the problem is skilled work, and it takes time whether or not the customer goes ahead.
  • The stocked truck. The parts and tools on board are why you can often fix it on the first visit. That inventory costs money to carry.
  • A share of overhead. Insurance, licensing, tools, and the phone that took the call.
  • The slot. An hour spent on a small call is an hour you can't sell to a bigger job.

How to set your service call fee

Build it from your own costs rather than copying the plumber down the road. Work out what one typical visit costs you to deliver: your travel, the time you'd normally spend diagnosing, and your overhead. Then price it at your target margin like any other job.

Here's a worked example. Your labor rate is $85 an hour. A typical call means a 15-mile drive, which you cost at $1.00 a mile, and about half an hour of diagnosis. You use 15% overhead and a 20% target margin.

Building a service call fee
Diagnosis (0.5 hour at $85)$42.50
Travel (15 miles at $1.00)$15.00
Subtotal$57.50
Overhead (15% of subtotal)$8.63
Cost basis$66.13
Service call fee (cost basis ÷ 0.80)$82.66

Illustrative example — figures chosen to show the method, not a quote. How we source figures.

Now see what happens without it. Bill that same quick fix by the minute, a fifteen-minute repair at $85 an hour, and it comes to $30.55 once overhead and margin are added, with no travel included. Add the 15-mile drive and it's still only $52.11, and that's before any time spent finding the fault. The service call fee is what lets a quick fix still pay for the trip.

A sanity check, not a target

For a rough sense of what plumbers charge for a service call, see the labeled marketplace estimate in our guide to plumber hourly rates. Use it to check your number against the market, but set yours from your own costs and your own area.

Credit it to the repair, or keep it?

Both approaches are common, and neither is wrong:

  • Credit it. If the customer goes ahead, the fee comes off the repair. It feels fair and encourages them to say yes, but it means the visit is effectively free when they proceed, so build that into your repair pricing.
  • Keep it separate. The visit and diagnosis are real work with real value, so they're charged either way. It's simpler, and it keeps your repair prices honest.

Pick one, put it in writing before the visit, and apply it the same way every time.

How to explain it to a customer

  • Say it on the phone, before you book. "There's a service call fee of $X that covers coming out and diagnosing the problem. If you go ahead with the repair, it's credited toward the work." One sentence, no surprise later.
  • Say what it buys. A licensed plumber, arriving with a stocked truck, who can usually fix it in one visit.
  • Be consistent. The same rule for everyone. Ad-hoc exceptions make the fee feel arbitrary.
  • Follow with a clear price. After diagnosing, give a firm number for the repair, ideally as a flat-rate quote, so the customer knows the total before you start.

After-hours and emergency calls

Late-night and weekend calls usually cost you more to deliver, and many plumbers charge a premium for them. Decide whether your emergency premium applies to the service call fee as well as to labor, and say so up front. The pricing side is covered in emergency and after-hours rates, and the wider picture is in how to price a plumbing job.

If you're the customer

If you're a homeowner wondering what this fee is, it's usually a charge for the plumber to come out and diagnose the problem, whether or not you then hire them for the repair. A few questions are worth asking before you book:

  • What does the fee include: the drive, the diagnosis, the first half-hour or hour of work?
  • Is it credited toward the repair if I go ahead?
  • What will I owe if I decide not to proceed after the diagnosis?
  • Is there an extra charge for evenings, weekends, or emergencies?

Ask for the answers in writing. For the wider picture of how plumbers price, see how much plumbers charge per hour.

The bottom line

Showing up and finding the problem is real work, and a service call fee is how you get paid for it. Build the fee from your drive, your diagnosis time, and your overhead. Decide whether to credit it to the repair, say it up front, and apply it the same way every time.

Common questions

What is a plumber's service call fee?

It's a set charge for a plumber to come out to a property and diagnose the problem, separate from the cost of the repair. It covers the drive, the time to find the fault, the stocked truck, and a share of the business's overhead. It's also called a call-out fee, trip charge, or diagnostic fee.

Is a service call fee the same as a call-out fee or trip charge?

Often the terms are used interchangeably, but they can differ. A service call or call-out fee usually covers the whole visit including diagnosis, a trip charge is often just the travel, and a diagnostic fee is specifically for finding the problem. Confirm what a given fee includes.

How much should I charge for a service call?

There's no standard figure. Work it out from your own costs: your travel time and fuel, the diagnosis time you'd normally spend, and a share of overhead, priced at your target margin. Then check the result against what plumbers in your area charge, using the labeled market estimate in our plumber rates guide as a sanity check.

Should the service call fee be credited toward the repair?

Both approaches are common. Crediting it makes the price feel fair and encourages customers to proceed, while keeping it separate treats the visit and diagnosis as work with value in themselves. Choose one, tell customers before the visit, and apply it consistently.

Do I still pay the service call fee if I don't go ahead with the repair?

Usually yes, because the fee pays for the visit and diagnosis, which have happened either way. What you owe depends on what was agreed beforehand, so ask what the fee includes and what it covers if you decline, and get the answer in writing.

Can I charge a service call fee if I fix the problem quickly?

Yes. The fee covers the drive, the truck, and the diagnosis, which cost you the same however quick the repair turns out to be. It's what stops a fast fix from losing money once travel is counted. Tell the customer about it before you book.
