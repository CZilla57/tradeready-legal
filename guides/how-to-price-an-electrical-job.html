How to price an electrical job

Electrical work is licensed, code-bound, and carries real liability - so it should never be priced like a handyman job. Here is how to price it properly: the service-call fee, flat-rate pricing per device, putting permits and inspections in the number, and why a licensed rate looks high (and is right), with a job costed end to end.

By the TradeReady team Published Aug 22, 2026 Last reviewed Aug 22, 2026 11 min read

A homeowner looks at a new outlet, sees a part that costs a few dollars, and cannot understand why the bill is what it is. That gap - between what the customer sees and what the work actually is - is the whole challenge of pricing electrical. The outlet is nothing. The licensed hands that install it to code, the van stocked to do it, the insurance behind it, and the responsibility if it ever fails are everything, and none of them show up in the parts bin.

Electrical isn't priced by different math from any other trade - it is the same four parts (labor, materials, overhead, and profit) behind every price. What makes it its own problem is that the materials are almost beside the point, the work is gated by permits and inspections, and the real product you sell is a licensed professional's judgment and accountability. Price those and the number makes sense; price the parts and you will always look expensive and still lose money. This guide walks through the pieces, then costs a real job end to end.

Why electrical pricing is its own problem

Three things set electrical apart. First, it is a licensed trade with real liability: work that is wrong doesn't just look bad, it can start a fire or hurt someone, and you carry that responsibility and warrant the work. Second, it is gated by permits and inspections - a lot of jobs legally can't just be done and left; they have to be pulled, inspected, and signed off, which adds fees, paperwork, and return trips. Third, the materials are trivial next to the skill, so a price built up from parts will always undervalue the job.

Put together, that means the doorway instinct - "it's just an outlet, call it fifty bucks" - is exactly backwards. You are not selling the outlet. You are selling the fact that it is safe, legal, and someone qualified stands behind it. Price electrical well and you are really pricing expertise, safety, and accountability.

The four parts, electrician-style

Every honest price is built from the same four blocks. If you have read how to price a job, this is the same skeleton - here is what each part looks like for electrical:

  1. Labor - your licensed time, including the parts customers never think about: diagnosis, pulling permits, and the return trip for inspection.
  2. Materials - devices, cable, breakers, boxes, and connectors, at cost plus a markup. Small in dollars, but the markup still covers sourcing and the stock you carry.
  3. Overhead - the share of your running costs the job carries, and it is heavy: license and continuing education, liability insurance, and a van stocked with hundreds of parts so you can finish in one trip.
  4. Profit - what the business earns after all three above are paid, including paying you. Your wage is a cost; profit sits on top.

Add the first three to get your cost. Add profit to get your price. The rest of this guide is about the electrical-specific costs a parts-based quote misses: the license, the permits, and the service call.

Licensing, permits, and inspections: priced in, not absorbed

Electrical work for hire is a licensed trade in most places, and much of it requires a permit and an inspection before it is legally complete. Those aren't red tape to swallow quietly - they are line items:

  • The permit fee - a real cost, passed through in the quote.
  • The time to pull it and do the paperwork - your hours, billed.
  • The inspection return trip - meeting an inspector almost always means coming back on their schedule, a second visit with its own drive.

Where a permit is required, the honest quote carries all three. Absorbing permit and inspection time to make a headline number smaller just donates your hours, and skipping required permits to win a job is a licensing and liability risk, never a saving. Requirements vary by jurisdiction and change - confirm yours with the local authority before you quote.

Service work vs. projects: two ways to price

Electrical splits into two kinds of work that price differently:

  • Service and repair - install a fixture, swap a panel breaker, add an outlet, hang a ceiling fan, chase a fault. Best handled with flat-rate pricing: a price book of set prices per task, so the customer gets a number up front and you get rewarded for speed and experience.
  • Projects and rough-in - new circuits, a panel upgrade, a remodel or addition. Priced as an estimate built from labor plus materials plus permits, because every one is a little different.

A flat-rate book isn't a shortcut around the math - it is the math, done once in advance. You price each common task properly (time times your cost per hour, plus the device, plus margin), write it down, and quote from the book. Build the book from your own numbers, not a competitor's, and revisit it as your costs change.

The service call: charge for turning up

Before any flat-rate task comes the service call - a set fee for driving out, carrying the stocked van, and diagnosing the problem with a licensed eye. Charge it. Free quotes and free diagnosis feel friendly and quietly train customers to use you as an unpaid advisor, and the trips that go nowhere come straight out of your week. Many electricians credit the service-call fee toward the job if the customer proceeds, which keeps it fair while still protecting the dead runs. It is the electrical version of the minimum every solo trade needs.

Why the stocked van earns its keep

A big part of what a good electrician sells is finishing in one visit. That only works because the van carries hundreds of parts - devices, breakers, connectors, cable - bought and stored ahead of time. That stock is money tied up and overhead to carry, and it is exactly what saves the customer a second trip charge. Price it into your overhead; it is a feature, not a free perk.

Why your rate looks high (and why that's correct)

Customers compare your hourly figure to their own wage and flinch. The number is high for reasons that are all real: the license and the training behind it, the liability insurance, the code knowledge that keeps a house safe, the warranty you stand behind, the stocked van, and the plain fact that if the work fails the consequences are serious and they are yours. You are not charging a wage; you are charging for a licensed business that carries risk most trades never touch. If you want the full walk-through of turning that into an hourly figure, see how much do electricians charge per hour and the general how much to charge per hour.

Worked example: add a dedicated circuit

Let's price a common small project - running a new dedicated circuit for an appliance: a new breaker, a cable run, an outlet, plus pulling a permit and returning for the inspection. We'll use a $45/hr take-home wage and $30/hr overhead (both higher than an unlicensed trade to carry the license, insurance, and stocked van), a 25% materials markup, and a 25% profit margin.

Say it comes to about five hours all in - the wiring itself, plus the permit paperwork and the return trip to meet the inspector - and the materials (breaker, cable, outlet, box, connectors) come to $60 at cost. The permit fee is passed through separately at cost.

Add a dedicated circuit, incl. permit paperwork & inspection trip · ~5 hours
Labor - 5 hrs × $45 wage$225.00
Overhead - 5 hrs × $30$150.00
Materials - $60 cost + 25% markup$75.00
Cost of the job$450.00
Your profit on the job$150.00

Illustrative example — figures chosen to show the method, not a quote. How we source figures.

So you quote $600 plus the permit fee at cost. Your wage is paid, the license and van are carried, the materials come back with a fair markup, and the business clears $150 - a true 25% margin on the price. To get there you divide, not add: cost ÷ (1 − margin), so $450 ÷ 0.75 = $600. Multiplying $450 by 1.25 would leave you a hair short of a real margin - the difference that markup vs. margin is all about.

Now see the leak. Price this like a handyman - "an outlet and a bit of wire, couple of hours, $200" - and you drop three hours of licensed labor and overhead, the permit paperwork, and the inspection trip. You would do the same code-compliant, warranted, inspected work for a third of what it costs you, and take on all the liability for free. The wire didn't get cheaper; you just stopped charging for everything that makes it legal and safe.

How electricians underprice (and how to stop)

  • Pricing the part, not the expertise. The device costs pennies; the licensed, insured, code-compliant work is the product. Price the knowledge and the risk.
  • Giving away the service call. Turning up and diagnosing is real, valuable work. Charge a service-call or diagnostic fee, and credit it to the job if they proceed.
  • Absorbing permit and inspection time. The fee, the paperwork, and the return trip are cost. Put all three in the quote where a permit is required.
  • Guessing instead of using a flat-rate book. Price your common tasks once, write them down, and quote from the book - built from your own costs, not a competitor's.
  • Apologizing for a licensed rate. The license, insurance, warranty, and liability are what the customer is really buying. Don't discount them to seem cheap.

The bottom line

Electrical pricing works the moment you stop pricing parts and start pricing the profession. The materials are trivial; the license, the code knowledge, the liability, the stocked van, and the permits and inspections are the job. Charge a service call for turning up, put the permit and inspection time in the number, price your common tasks into a flat-rate book, and carry your real overhead. Do that and the rate that looked high to the customer turns out to be exactly what it costs to do electrical work safely, legally, and profitably.

Common questions

Do electricians charge by the hour or a flat rate per job?

Both, and most residential service electricians lean on flat-rate pricing for standard work - a set price to install an outlet, a switch, a fixture, or a ceiling fan - because customers want the total up front and it rewards you for being fast and skilled. Open-ended fault-finding and larger projects are often better hourly or as a project estimate. Whichever you show, build it the same way underneath: your cost per hour plus materials plus your margin. A flat-rate price is that math done in advance and kept in a price book, not a number guessed at the door.

What is a service call or diagnostic fee, and should I charge one?

A service-call or diagnostic fee is a set charge for turning up and finding out what is wrong, before any repair. Charge one. Driving to the property, carrying a stocked van, and using your licensed expertise to diagnose a fault is real work with real value, and giving away free quotes and diagnosis trains customers to shop you for nothing. Many electricians roll the fee into the job if the customer goes ahead, but it protects you against the trips that lead nowhere.

How do I price permits and inspections into an electrical job?

Treat them as real cost, because they are. Where the work requires a permit, price in the permit fee itself, the time to pull it and to complete the paperwork, and the return trip to meet the inspector - inspections almost always mean coming back on someone else's schedule. Never quietly absorb permit and inspection time to make a number look lower; it is hours and money out of your pocket, and cutting corners on required permits is a licensing and liability risk, not a saving.

Why do electricians charge so much more than the parts cost?

Because you are paying for the electrician, not the outlet. A device might cost a few dollars, but the price covers a licensed pro who knows the code, carries insurance and a stocked van, takes on the liability if the work is wrong, warrants it, and can be held responsible for a fire or a shock if it fails. The materials are almost never the point in electrical work - the expertise, the safety, and the accountability are. Price the knowledge and the risk, not the part.

Do I need a license to do electrical work for pay?

In most places, yes - electrical work for hire is a licensed trade, and doing it without the required license is a legal and insurance risk as well as a safety one. Requirements, permit rules, and what a given license level lets you do all vary by state, province, or local authority, and change over time. Confirm your own licensing and permit obligations with your local authority before you quote, and price the work at a level that reflects the license and liability you carry.
