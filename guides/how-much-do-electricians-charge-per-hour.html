How much do electricians charge per hour?

Independent electricians commonly charge somewhere around $75 to $130 an hour. But licensed electrical work carries costs a general handyman never sees - and your rate has to carry all of them. Here is how to set it.

By the TradeReady team Published Aug 19, 2026 Last reviewed Aug 19, 2026 8 min read

Ask what electricians charge and you'll hear everything from $60 to $150 an hour. The gap isn't one of them overcharging - it's the difference between a journeyman doing simple work in a low-cost area and a master electrician carrying permits, inspections, and a fully stocked service van in an expensive city.

This guide gives you a realistic range to check against, then walks the method for building your own rate. It's the same approach as our general guide to setting an hourly rate and the whole-job version in how to price a job, pointed at electrical.

The typical range for an independent electrician

As a market check, solo and small-shop electricians in the US tend to sit here:

Typical independent electrician pricing (US)
Hourly labor rate$75–$130 / hr
Service-call / diagnostic fee$75–$150
Emergency or after-hours1.5×–2× rate
Permit and inspection timebilled to the job

A journeyman handling receptacles, fixtures, and small circuits sits toward the bottom. A master electrician doing panel swaps, service upgrades, and EV-charger installs sits toward the top. Where you land is set by your license level, your costs, and local demand - which is what the next section builds.

Why licensed electrical work runs higher

An electrician's rate has to cover more than a hand with a screwdriver. Before any of it is wage, the number carries:

  • A journeyman or master license - years of training and continuing education to earn and keep.
  • Higher liability insurance - electrical work carries fire and shock risk, and it's priced accordingly.
  • Permits and inspections - pulling the permit, meeting the inspector, and the wait time between are real hours the job has to pay for.
  • A stocked service van and test gear - wire, breakers, meters, and the tools to work safely on live systems.
  • Unbillable hours - driving, quoting, code research, parts runs, and chasing payment.

A solo electrician might work 45 hours a week and bill only 25 to 30. Every cost above has to be recovered across those billable hours, which is exactly why the charge-out rate runs well over the wage the electrician actually takes home.

Build your rate from your own costs

The short version (the full billable-hours math is in how much to charge per hour):

  1. Set your take-home target - the annual wage this business owes you.
  2. Count your real billable hours - the hours you invoice, not the hours you work. Often 1,000 to 1,300 a year for a solo operator.
  3. Total your yearly overhead - license, insurance, van, fuel, tools, software, phone - and spread it across those billable hours.
  4. Wage per hour plus overhead per hour = your cost rate - your break-even floor.
  5. Add profit on top. The margin above cost is the only part that gets you ahead.
Why licensed trades sit higher

Our general example lands a solo operator at a $60/hr cost rate. A licensed electrician usually lands higher - the insurance is dearer, permits and inspections eat billable time, and the license itself represents years of cost. That's not padding; it's a higher genuine cost of doing the work legally and safely, and your rate has to carry it.

Charge a service call and bill permit time

A pure hourly rate loses money on small jobs and hides real costs on big ones. Two habits fix that:

  • A service-call or diagnostic fee covers the trip and the assessment so a short visit still pays. Some electricians credit it toward the job if the customer goes ahead.
  • Bill permit and inspection time explicitly. Pulling the permit, waiting for the inspector, and the return trip are hours the job consumes. If they're not on the estimate, you're absorbing them.

Flat-rate vs. hourly for common jobs

Familiar jobs - a panel upgrade, a ceiling-fan install, adding a circuit, an EV charger - are often quoted flat rather than hourly. Customers like the certainty, and once you've done the job a dozen times you know the hours. Flat pricing is still built on your hourly cost rate: estimate the labor honestly, multiply by the rate you built above, add materials with a sensible markup and the permit cost, and you have a fixed number that still pays. The bottom line

"How much do electricians charge per hour?" has no single answer, because the number is the output of one electrician's license level, costs, and billable hours. Build yours from a real wage plus real overhead divided by the hours you can invoice, add profit, charge a service call, and bill permit time openly. Use the market range only to confirm you're in the right ballpark - never to set your price.

Common questions

How much do electricians charge per hour?

Independent electricians in the US commonly charge somewhere around $75 to $130 an hour, with a service-call or minimum fee on top. A journeyman running straightforward work sits lower; a master electrician doing panel and service upgrades sits higher. The rate that matters is the one built from your own costs.

Why do electricians charge more than a handyman?

Electrical work is licensed, permitted, inspected, and carries real liability. An electrician's rate has to cover a journeyman or master license, higher insurance, permit and inspection time, and the code knowledge that keeps the work safe and legal. Those are genuine costs a general handyman does not carry.

Do electricians charge for a quote or call-out?

Many charge a service-call or diagnostic fee that covers the trip and the assessment, sometimes credited toward the job if the customer proceeds. Larger jobs are often quoted as a flat price built from the same hourly cost rate plus materials and permit costs.
