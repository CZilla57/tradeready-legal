How much do electricians charge per hour?

As a rough US ballpark, independent electricians often bill somewhere around $75 to $130 an hour - but the hourly rate is only one line on the bill. This guide explains how electrician pricing actually fits together, whether you're comparing quotes or setting your own rate.

By the TradeReady team Published Aug 19, 2026 Last reviewed Aug 22, 2026 10 min read

Ask what electricians charge and you'll hear anything from $60 to $150 an hour. The gap isn't one of them overcharging - it's the difference between simple work in a low-cost area and permitted, inspected work carried out of a fully stocked van in an expensive city. The hourly rate isn't a price tag; it's one input into a bill that usually also carries a service-call fee, materials, permit and inspection costs, and sometimes a flat price that ignores the clock entirely.

Who this guide is for

This page serves two readers, in clearly labeled sections. If you're a customer comparing electrical quotes, start with what electricians charge and why the bill works the way it does. If you're an independent electrician setting your rate, skip to building a rate that actually pays. Everyone is welcome in both.

Those are the shape of the market, not your quote. Where an electrician lands is set by their costs, the complexity of the job, and local demand - not by their license title on its own. A straightforward repair in a rural area sits lower; permitted, inspected work out of a fully stocked van in a high-cost city sits higher. What ends up on your invoice depends on the job and the shop.

The parts of an electrical bill, and what each one means

Almost every confusing-looking electrical invoice makes sense once you know the pieces. They're separate charges, and not every job has all of them:

  • Labor rate - the per-hour price for time on the tools. It's the number people ask about, but rarely the whole bill.
  • Service-call fee (also call-out, trip, or diagnostic fee) - a fixed charge for coming out and assessing the problem. It pays for the drive and the electrician's time to figure out what's wrong.
  • Minimum charge - the smallest amount a shop will bill for a visit, so a short fix still covers the cost of getting a licensed electrician to your door.
  • Flat-rate price - a fixed quote for a known job (a panel swap, adding a circuit, a fixture install) regardless of how long it takes. You get certainty; the clock stops mattering.
  • Materials - wire, breakers, devices, and fixtures, usually billed with a markup that covers sourcing, stocking, and warranty. (Markup and margin aren't the same thing - see markup vs. margin.)
  • Permits and inspections - a lot of electrical work legally requires a pulled permit and an inspector's sign-off; the permit fee and the time to handle it are passed through to you. More on how that appears on the bill below.
  • Emergency premium - the extra charged for off-hours or urgent response, commonly 1.5× to 2× the standard rate.

When permit and inspection time is in the price - and when it's a separate line

Two different things get bundled under the word "permit," and they appear on the bill differently:

  • The permit fee itself is money paid to the city or county. It's almost always passed straight through as its own line, at cost.
  • The time to handle permits and inspections - filling out the application, scheduling the inspector, waiting on site, and often a separate return visit once the inspector can come - is labor. On a flat-rate job, that time is frequently already built into the fixed price, so you won't see a line for it. On an hourly or time-and-materials job, it's usually itemized separately as billed hours, including the return trip.

Neither approach is wrong; they're just two ways of accounting for the same real work. What matters is that you ask, up front, whether permit and inspection handling is included in the quoted price or billed on top - so a return visit for the inspector doesn't arrive as a surprise line later.

Why your bill isn't "hours on site × hourly rate"

A common frustration: the electrician was at your house for 40 minutes, the rate is $110/hr, so why is the bill $211? Because you can almost never rebuild an electrical invoice by multiplying visible onsite time by the hourly rate. The total also carries things you didn't watch happen:

  • a service-call or minimum charge that's a fixed amount, not a fraction of an hour;
  • flat-rate pricing on jobs where the clock is deliberately ignored;
  • materials - wire, breakers, and devices - with markup;
  • permit fees and inspection handling, including a return trip for the inspector;
  • billed travel, a parts run, or code research - time spent for your job that happened off your property.

None of that means you were overcharged. It means the hourly rate was only ever one line of several. If a total surprises you, ask for the invoice broken out by these parts - a reputable electrician will walk you through it.

Licensing, journeyman, and master (varies by where you live)

Part of what an electrician's rate carries is the cost of being licensed and insured - and hiring one who is protects you if something goes wrong. The terms are worth understanding, but they're less fixed than they sound. Broadly, apprentice, journeyman, and master describe stages of training and responsibility. But the exact classifications, the exam and experience each requires, and what each level is permitted to do - including who may pull a permit or run a company - are set by each state, and sometimes by the county or city, not by a single national rule. Some places license electricians at the state level; others regulate locally; the license classes and their allowed scopes differ from one place to the next.

A few things follow from that. A master's license doesn't automatically mean higher prices, and it doesn't mean that person always does the panel work while a journeyman only changes receptacles - who does what depends on the shop, the job, and local rules. What's consistent is worth doing before you hire: for any work that must be permitted and inspected, use a licensed electrician, ask for a license number and proof of insurance, and verify both with your state or local licensing authority. Treat any general statement here as a prompt to check locally, not a definitive rule.

For electricians: building a rate that pays

If you're setting your own rate, the market range above is a sanity-check, not a target. The rate that matters is the one built from your costs. The full method - with the billable-hours math - is in our general guide to setting an hourly rate; this section points it at electrical rather than repeating it.

Why electrical rates look high (they mostly aren't)

Customers see "$110 an hour" and picture the electrician pocketing $110. They don't see what the number has to cover before any of it is wage. Electrical carries a set of costs that a general handyman rate never has to:

  • Permits and inspections - pulling permits, scheduling inspectors, waiting on site, and the return visits for sign-off are hours the business absorbs unless the rate carries them.
  • Continuing education and licensing - the license that lets you legally do the work, kept current, plus the code-update classes many jurisdictions require to renew it.
  • Test equipment - multimeters, clamp meters, insulation testers, voltage detectors, and receptacle testers, bought and periodically recalibrated or replaced.
  • Code research - time spent confirming what the current code cycle and local amendments require for a given job, before and sometimes during the work.
  • Service-van inventory - wire, breakers, devices, boxes, and connectors carried on the truck so you fix it on the first visit instead of a second parts run.
  • Return inspection visits - a distinct trip, often on the inspector's schedule rather than yours, that earns nothing extra but costs a slot in the day.
  • Liability insurance - non-negotiable, and priced for the fire and shock risk the work carries.
  • Protective equipment and safe-work practice - insulated tools, gloves, and other PPE, plus the time to test for and confirm de-energization, lock out and tag the source, and work in compliance with the safety requirements that apply. That testing, diagnostic, and lockout time is part of doing the job correctly, and the rate has to cover it.
  • Unbillable hours - driving, quoting, code research, parts runs, and chasing payment. None of it bills; all of it is time.

That last one is the big one. An electrician might work a 45-hour week but bill only 25 to 30 hours of it. Every dollar of wage, insurance, test gear, and van stock has to be recovered across those billable hours - so the rate per billable hour has to run well above the wage you take home.

Build the rate from your own costs

The short version (the full walkthrough, with the billable-hours math, is in how much to charge per hour): take your yearly take-home target, add your yearly overhead, and divide the total by the hours you can actually bill - not the hours you work. That gives your cost rate, the floor. Charge exactly it and you break even; the profit is whatever you add on top.

Why licensed trades sit higher

Our general example lands a solo operator at a $60/hr cost rate. A licensed electrician usually lands higher - the insurance is dearer, permits and inspections eat billable time, test equipment and van stock cost money to buy and maintain, and continuing education is a recurring cost. That's not padding; it's a higher genuine cost of doing the work legally and safely, and your rate has to carry it.

Charge a service call, and bill permit and inspection time

A pure hourly rate punishes you on small jobs and hides real costs on big ones. Two habits fix that:

  • A service-call or diagnostic fee covers the trip and the assessment so a short visit still pays. Decide clearly whether that fee includes a first block of labor or sits beside it, and put it in the quote - unclear fee structures are exactly what causes the customer confusion in the section above. Some electricians credit the fee toward the job if the customer goes ahead.
  • Account for permit and inspection time explicitly. Pulling the permit, waiting for the inspector, and the return trip are hours the job consumes. On a flat-rate quote, build them into the number; on a time-and-materials job, itemize them as billed hours. Either way, tell the customer which you're doing - if that time isn't in the price, you're absorbing it.

Flat-rate vs. hourly for common jobs

Plenty of electricians quote familiar jobs - a panel upgrade, a ceiling-fan install, adding a circuit, an EV charger - as a flat price rather than by the hour. The customer likes the certainty, and once you've done a job a dozen times you know the hours. Flat-rate pricing is still built from your hourly cost rate underneath: estimate the labor honestly, multiply by the rate you built above, add materials with a sensible markup and the permit cost, and you have a fixed number that still pays. The bottom line

"How much do electricians charge per hour?" has no single answer, and the hourly rate alone never tells you the whole cost. For customers, the bill is the labor rate plus a service call, materials, and permit and inspection costs - which is why you can't rebuild it from onsite minutes, and why it's worth asking whether inspection time is in the price or billed on top. For electricians, the honest rate is a real wage plus real overhead - licensing, insurance, test gear, van stock, code research, and inspection trips - divided by the hours you can actually bill, with profit on top. Use the market range only to check you're in the right ballpark, never to set the price for you.

Common questions

How much do electricians charge per hour?

As a rough US ballpark, independent electricians often bill somewhere around $75 to $130 an hour, and many add a service-call or minimum fee on top. This is TradeReady's own marketplace estimate reviewed in August 2026, not a formal survey, and the figure an electrician charges a customer already has to cover licensing, insurance, test equipment, a stocked van, permit and inspection time, and unbillable hours - it is not a take-home wage. Your actual quote depends on the job, your location, and the electrician's costs, not on their license title alone.

What is an electrician's service-call or call-out fee?

A service-call fee (also called a call-out, trip, or diagnostic fee) is a fixed charge for coming to your property and assessing the problem, commonly in the $75 to $150 range as a rough US estimate. On many jobs it covers the first block of time as well, so it does not always stack on top of a full hourly rate from the first minute - ask the electrician how their fee and hourly rate interact before work starts.

Why is my electrical bill more than the hours on site times the hourly rate?

Because the hourly rate is only one line of the bill. A typical invoice can also include a service-call or minimum charge, a flat-rate price for the job rather than clock time, materials with markup, permit and inspection fees, and billed travel, code research, or a return trip for the inspector that you did not see. Multiplying the minutes on site by the hourly rate almost never reproduces the total, and that is normal rather than a sign of overcharging. Ask for the invoice broken out by these parts.

Do electricians charge more for emergencies or after hours?

Usually, yes. Nights, weekends, holidays, and true emergency call-outs are commonly billed at roughly 1.5 to 2 times the standard rate, a rough estimate that varies by shop. The premium pays for interrupting off-hours and responding fast. If the situation is safe to wait - and a burning smell, sparking, or a hot panel is not - asking about standard-rate scheduling can lower the cost.

What do journeyman and master electrician mean, and do I need a licensed one?

Broadly, apprentice, journeyman, and master describe stages of training and responsibility, but the exact classifications, the exam and experience required, and what each level is permitted to do - including who may pull a permit or run a company - are set by each state and sometimes the county or city, not by one national rule. A master's license does not automatically mean higher prices or a particular kind of job. For work that must be permitted and inspected, use a licensed electrician and verify the license and insurance with your local licensing authority; what is required depends entirely on your jurisdiction.
