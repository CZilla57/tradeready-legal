What to put in a job estimate (with a free template)

A clear estimate does two jobs: it helps you win the work, and it lowers the odds of an argument later about what was included. Below is a practical nine-part starting checklist - and a template you can copy today. Treat it as a general example, not a ready-made legal contract: what a valid estimate or contract must contain varies by location.

By the TradeReady team Published Aug 18, 2026 Last reviewed Aug 20, 2026 11 min read

You've priced the job properly. Now you have to put a number in front of the customer in a way that looks professional, sets expectations if the job changes, and makes it easy to say yes. A scribbled figure on a text message does none of those things.

This guide assumes you already have your price. If you don't yet, start with how to price a job and come back - an estimate is just the document that carries your number to the customer.

Read this first

This is an educational guide and a template to adapt - not legal advice, and not a finished contract. Contract, licensing, deposit, and consumer-protection rules differ by country, state, and trade, and they change. Before you rely on any wording here in a customer agreement, check the rules where you work, and get local legal advice if the job or the amount warrants it.

Estimate, quote, invoice: what people usually mean

Three words that get used loosely. Here's how tradespeople generally use them - but note that the words themselves don't decide the legal effect. What a document does depends on your jurisdiction, the exact wording, and how both sides act on it:

  • Estimate - usually a best-informed projection of the cost, understood to move if the work turns out bigger than expected. Often treated as a good-faith figure rather than a firm commitment - but see the note on binding effect below.
  • Quote - usually meant as a fixed price for a defined scope. If you present it that way and the customer accepts, you'll generally be expected to hold it, so your scope had better be tight.
  • Invoice - the bill, sent after the work (or a stage of it) is done. It's a request for payment, not a proposal.

Whichever you send, say which one it is on the document, and state plainly whether the number is fixed or approximate and what would change it. Being explicit is the part that actually helps - a line like "This is an estimate; the final price may change if the scope below changes" sets expectations, though it's no substitute for a clear written agreement where one is needed.

On binding effect

Don't assume an estimate can never bind you. Depending on how it's worded, how the customer accepts it, and the law where you work, an accepted estimate can sometimes become a binding agreement - especially if it reads like a firm offer. If you mean the price to be able to move, label it an estimate and say so; if you mean it to be firm, call it a fixed quote. When certainty matters, have the wording checked locally.

A practical nine-part starting checklist

These nine parts cover most professional estimates. Treat them as a starting point and add the extra fields in the next section wherever your trade or location calls for them:

  1. Your business details - and license or registration info where required. Name, logo, phone, and email. Many jurisdictions require a licensed or registered contractor to show that number on estimates and contracts, so include it where your trade demands it.
  2. An estimate number and date. So you can both refer to it later, and so you can track which estimates turned into work.
  3. The customer and job address - with contact details. Who it's for, their phone and email, and where the work happens - which aren't always the same.
  4. A clear scope of work. What you will actually do, in plain language. This is the part that does the most to set expectations (more below).
  5. Exclusions. What is not included. Just as important as the scope, and far more often forgotten.
  6. A price breakdown - including tax. Labor, materials, and any line items, then a subtotal, any applicable tax, and a total. You don't have to bare your margin, but the customer should see what they're buying and whether tax is included or added.
  7. Any deposit required. How much up front and when the balance is due - after checking your local rules on deposits (see below).
  8. Payment terms and a schedule. What you accept, by when, and an estimated start window or timeline so expectations line up.
  9. An expiry date, a way to accept, and a signature line with a date. How long the price holds, how they say yes, and space for a signature and acceptance date.

Fields to add where they apply

Beyond the nine basics, these often matter - and in some places are required. Include the ones that fit your trade, job, and location:

  • License / registration number. Required on contractor documents in many jurisdictions - check your licensing board.
  • Insurance and bond information. Some work, or some customers, require proof of liability insurance or a bond; some jurisdictions require it to be stated.
  • Permits and who obtains them. Say who pulls and pays for any required permits - you or the customer. Permit requirements vary by location and job type.
  • Warranties. What you stand behind and for how long (for example, labor for a set period). Be specific, and don't promise more than you'll honor.
  • Change-order approval. State that any change to the scope is quoted separately and approved - ideally in writing - before that work begins.
  • Cancellation rights. Where applicable, some jurisdictions give consumers a right to cancel within a set window (for example, certain home-solicitation or right-to-cancel rules). If that applies to your work, include the required notice.
  • Taxes. Show whether sales/labor tax applies and whether it's included in or added to the total.

How to write scope so you don't work for free

Scope creep - "while you're here, could you also..." - is where profit quietly disappears. A clear estimate is your best defense, and vague scope is what leaves the door open. Compare:

Vague vs. tight

Weak: "Paint the bathroom."
Tight: "Prep and paint bathroom walls and ceiling (approx. 120 sq ft), two coats, customer's supplied color. Excludes trim, door, tiling repairs, and any drywall work beyond minor filling."

The tight version tells the customer exactly what they're getting and gives you a clean line to point at when the extra request comes: "Happy to - that's outside the estimate, so let me price it as a change." You're not being difficult; you're holding to what you both agreed.

Deposits and expiry dates

Two small fields that carry real weight:

  • Deposit. For anything with material costs or a multi-day timeline, a deposit up front covers your outlay and filters out tire-kickers. But don't just pick a percentage: some states and countries limit how much a contractor can take up front, or restrict deposits for certain work. Check the rules where you operate first, then set an amount that matches the materials you have to buy before you start.
  • Expiry date. Prices move - materials especially. "Valid for 30 days" protects you from someone accepting a six-month-old number after your costs have climbed, and it gently nudges a wavering customer to decide.

Fixed-quote version

Selling a firm price for a fully defined scope? Swap the PRICE and TERMS blocks above for these, and rename the heading from ESTIMATE to QUOTE:

Fixed-quote version · price & terms blocks
PRICE (FIXED QUOTE)
  Total price ........................ $[ ]
  Tax [rate / if applicable] ............. $[ ]
  QUOTED TOTAL ....................... $[ ]
  (fixed for the scope above, until the valid-until date)

TERMS
  This is a fixed quote for the scope listed above. The price holds
  until the valid-until date. Work outside that scope is a separate,
  pre-approved charge. A signed, accepted quote may form a binding
  agreement depending on its wording and the law where you work.

Use the range version when the job could shift; use the fixed-quote version only when the scope is fully defined and you're prepared to hold the number.

When they go quiet: following up

Most estimates don't get a no. They get silence. A single, friendly follow-up wins a surprising amount of work that would otherwise drift away:

  • Wait 2 to 3 days, then check in once. Any sooner reads as pushy; much later and they've booked someone else.
  • Keep it short and useful. "Hi [name], just checking you got the estimate for [job] - happy to walk through anything or tweak the scope. It's held until [date]."
  • Let the expiry do the work. The validity date gives you a natural, no-pressure reason to follow up without nagging.
  • One nudge is usually enough. If there's still nothing after a second touch, move on - and keep the estimate on file in case they come back.

The bottom line

A good estimate is more than paperwork - it helps you win the job cleanly and keeps everyone on the same page about what was agreed. Cover the nine parts, add the extra fields your trade and location call for, write scope tight enough to protect your profit, put an expiry on it, and follow up once. And because deposit limits, licensing, and contract rules vary from place to place, check what applies where you work before you rely on any template - including this one.

Common questions

What is the difference between an estimate and a quote?

In everyday trade use, an estimate is a best-informed projection of what a job will cost and can move if the work changes, while a quote is usually meant as a fixed price for a defined scope. But these words are used loosely, and their legal effect depends on your jurisdiction, the exact wording of your document, and how both sides actually behave. What matters most is being clear in writing about whether the number is fixed or approximate, and what would change it.

Can an estimate become legally binding?

Sometimes. An estimate is often treated as a good-faith projection rather than a firm contract, but an accepted estimate can become a binding agreement depending on its wording, the parties' conduct, and the law where you work - for example if it reads like a fixed offer and the customer accepts it. Because this varies, state plainly on the document whether the figure is an estimate or a fixed quote, note what would change the price, and check the rules in your area if you need certainty.

How much of a deposit can I ask for?

A deposit helps cover materials you buy before you start, but some states and countries cap how much a contractor may take up front, or limit deposits for certain kinds of work. Check the rules where you operate before you settle on a percentage or amount, rather than assuming a common figure like a third is allowed everywhere.

Do I have to put my license or registration number on an estimate?

It depends on your trade and location. Many jurisdictions require licensed or registered contractors to show that number on estimates and contracts, and some also require specific insurance, bond, or cancellation-rights language. Check your local licensing board's rules and include whatever it requires.

Who is responsible for pulling permits?

That is a decision to make and write down, not a fixed rule. On some jobs the contractor pulls and pays for permits; on others the customer does. Whatever you agree, state clearly on the estimate who obtains any required permits and who pays for them, so there is no surprise later. Permit requirements themselves vary by location and job type.
